FROM SPARK
TO FLAME

How a raw concept becomes a full narrative. Breaking down the development process from first idea to structured story.

Every project starts somewhere. For us, it rarely starts with a polished pitch or a clean three-act structure. It starts with a feeling — something half-formed that keeps showing back up. This article is about what we do with that.

The gap between "I have this idea" and "I have something we can actually make" is where most projects die. Not because the idea wasn't good enough, but because there's no clear process for crossing that gap. Here's ours.

Step One: Don't Kill It Early

The first instinct when you have a raw idea is to test it. You tell someone, you pitch it, you ask if it's any good. This is usually a mistake. Ideas at the spark stage are fragile — they can't survive contact with skepticism yet because you haven't built the structure around them that makes them defensible.

Give it time to breathe. Write down what it is without trying to make it better. Just describe it. What's the feeling? What's the image? What's the thing you keep coming back to? That's your seed.

Step Two: Find the Spine

Once you have the seed, the next question is: what is this actually about? Not the plot, not the characters — the thematic core. What question does this story want to ask?

For Rue's Ritual, the spine was something like: what do people keep doing even when they know it won't work? That question shaped everything — character decisions, tone, even visual style. When you have that question, everything else has something to push against.

Step Three: The First Ugly Draft

Now you write. And it will be bad. That's the point. The first draft is about getting the shape of the thing on paper — not the quality of it. Structure first, voice second. You can't edit nothing.

What we actually do

We use a simple beat sheet before any formal script work. Each beat is a single sentence: what happens, and what changes because of it. If something doesn't change something, it doesn't belong yet.

  • Write the opening image — what's the first thing we see?
  • Write the break — what disrupts the world?
  • Write the turn — what does the protagonist choose?
  • Write the end — how has something changed?

That's it for round one. Four sentences. From there you expand outward.

Development is messy and non-linear and uncomfortable. But if you can protect the spark long enough to find the spine, the rest becomes craft. And craft can be learned.
